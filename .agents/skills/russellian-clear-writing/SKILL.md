# Russellian Clear Writing

Use this skill to write, revise, or evaluate prose whose main purpose is explanation, argument, or analysis.

The aim is not to imitate Bertrand Russell's mannerisms. Do not produce Edwardian diction, fake philosophical grandeur, or ornamental solemnity. Preserve the virtues of his best expository prose: clarity, economy, proportion, and logical order.

## Governing Principle

Make the thought transparent. The force of the text should come from the reasoning, not from display.

## Russell's Tests for Expository Prose

Russell's advice in "How I Write" can be turned into three practical tests.

1. **"Never use a long word if a short word will do."**  
   Prefer the shorter word when it says the same thing.

2. **"If you want to make a statement with a great many qualifications, put some of the qualifications in separate sentences."**  
   Separate conditions, limits, and exceptions instead of overloading one sentence.

3. **"Do not let the beginning of your sentence lead the reader to an expectation which is contradicted by the end."**  
   Do not betray the reader's expectation.

Apply these tests sentence by sentence, but do not make the prose mechanical.

## Style Commitments

### Clarity Before Ornament

Use direct sentences. Let elegance arise from precision and rhythm, not decoration.

Avoid:

- decorative adjectives;
- unnecessary metaphors;
- solemn language without content;
- technical terms used to impress;
- sentences whose structure hides the main claim.

Prefer:

- exact words;
- active constructions when natural;
- concrete verbs;
- sentences with one clear task.

### Plain Language Without Simplism

A text may handle difficult ideas, but it should not make them more difficult than they are.

Use technical terms only when they are needed. Define them before relying on them. Do not confuse depth with obscurity.

### Analytical Order

A paragraph should advance one step in the argument.

When useful, organize the movement as follows:

1. state the issue;
2. define the relevant term;
3. separate claim, assumption, and implication;
4. examine the consequence;
5. state the conclusion.

This order is not mandatory. Use it when it helps the reader follow the reasoning.

### Sober Argument

Argue with reasons, not noise.

The tone may be firm, but it should not be theatrical. Criticism should expose a weakness by making it clear, not by exaggerating it.

Irony, if used, should be restrained. Let the absurdity of an idea appear through accurate exposition.

### Public Intelligibility

Write as if an educated reader deserves to understand the matter without artificial difficulty.

Do not write to appear more complex than necessary. Write so that the real difficulty of the subject can be seen.

## Revision Rule

Revise substance without hesitation. If the argument is wrong, incomplete, or badly ordered, rewrite it.

Revise form with restraint. Do not polish a sentence merely to make it sound more literary if it already says the right thing clearly.

A useful revision sequence is:

1. Check whether the claim is true or adequately supported.
2. Check whether the order of ideas is logical.
3. Check whether any sentence is overloaded.
4. Replace inflated words with exact ones.
5. Stop when further polishing only changes the sound, not the sense.

## Logical Distinctions

Mark distinctions when they matter. Do not rely on stock phrases.

Instead of using fixed templates, ask:

- Is this a claim, an inference, or a possibility?
- Does this conclusion require a condition?
- Is there an exception the reader must know?
- Could the reader confuse cause, evidence, and consequence?

Make the distinction explicit in natural language.

## Practical Review Checklist

Before finalizing the text, ask:

- Can any long word be replaced by a shorter exact word?
- Does any sentence carry too many conditions, limits, or exceptions?
- Does any sentence begin by creating an expectation that its ending contradicts?
- Does each paragraph advance one clear step?
- Are the key terms defined before they are used heavily?
- Are the main claims supported by reasons or evidence?
- Has any decorative phrase survived without adding meaning?
- Are there stacked adjectives that say nearly the same thing?
- Is any connector present only as habit rather than logic?
- Has revision improved the thought, or only altered the sound?

## Optional Structure for Documents and Reports

For reports, essays, and academic documents, an orderly structure is often useful:

1. **Introduction**  
   State the subject, purpose, and relevance.

2. **Problem or question**  
   Explain what is being examined and what is outside the scope.

3. **Analysis**  
   Develop the argument in logical steps.

4. **Distinctions and limits**  
   Separate concepts, causes, implications, and uncertainties that may be confused.

5. **Conclusion**  
   Summarize the result without exaggeration or new claims.

This is a default structure, not a law. If an anecdote, example, or provocation makes the argument clearer, it may come first.

## Quality Standard

A text satisfies this skill if the reader can identify:

- the question being addressed;
- the terms being used;
- the claim being defended;
- the reasons supporting it;
- the limits of the conclusion;
- the order of the argument.

## Guiding Sentence

Write so that, once the reasoning has been explained, it seems almost inevitable.
